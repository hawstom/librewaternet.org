The whole list, plainly, so you can search it for the thing you need, tell us what is missing from it, or measure it against whatever you use today.

This is a list of what has been built, not of what is planned. Everything on it works today, in a browser, free, with no account. It covers the network model, and the single-purpose hydraulic calculators kept alongside it by the same author. The list is incomplete: a line is on it because somebody wrote a sentence for it, and plenty of smaller work never got one. If you only want to know whether the thing is any good, diving in saves time.

62 things it does. And what it does not.

The calculators 12

  • Manning pipe flow solves in both directions: the flow from a depth, and the depth from a flow.
  • Every pipe and channel page checks the velocity it just computed and says when it is too fast or too slow.
  • Orifice flow, and the time a tank takes to drain through one.
  • Micro-hydropower, from gross head and penstock losses to the power a small site can actually deliver.
  • Canal seepage and conveyance efficiency, including whether lining the canal pays for itself.
  • Rock chute sizing by the Robinson method.
  • Sketches drawn to scale from your own numbers, so you can see the section you just described.
  • A tip on every input saying what the number is, in the language of the page.
  • One button returns a page to a worked example that passes, so nothing is ever a blank form.
  • Name a calculation and copy a link to it; the link carries your inputs, and nothing is shared unless you share it.
  • Changing an input unit asks first, and says plainly that it reinterprets the numbers you typed rather than converting them.
  • Install it from the browser you already have, and every calculator keeps working with no network at all.

Drawing a water network 15

  • A drawing toolbar (junction, reservoir, tank, pipe, pump, valve and text), placed by clicking the map.
  • Many named projects, saved in your own browser, each open in its own tab.
  • The property box is a window you can drag out of your way, not a panel that owns an edge of the screen.
  • ID prefixes you choose, with Apply to all for when you change your mind.
  • Find elements by any property, jump straight to one by ID, and set a property across everything the search found.
  • Or type the search out yourself, with AND, OR and brackets, when what you are looking for is more than the pull-downs can say, in the words of your own language.
  • The File menu remembers your recent projects, and the map remembers where you were looking.
  • One toggle clears the readouts off the map when you want the drawing and nothing else.
  • The menus follow the work: everything about the water system is under Water, and everything drawn behind and around it is under Map, so a control is found by name rather than hunted for.
  • A long Settings box is searchable and indexed: type a word and only the rows that mention it stay, or jump straight to any section from the list beside them.
  • Fire flow analysis for the whole system in one press: every junction tested against the flow your code requires, what it can actually deliver at the residual you set, and what drawing that flow does to everything around it.
  • Water quality over a run: how old the water reaching a point is, how much of it came from a source you name, and how a chemical residual decays as it travels, with the reaction coefficients yours to state rather than ours to guess.
  • What the pumps cost: how long each one ran, the power it drew, the energy it used and the money that came to, including the demand charge on the highest power drawn at any one moment.
  • A picture of your own under the drawing, a site plan or an aerial, scaled by picking two points on it or by typing the size of a pixel.
  • A Help menu specific to this calculator.

Solving it 4

  • A looped network solved by the global gradient method, in the page, with no install and no upload.
  • Or solved by the EPANET 2.3.5 engine itself, compiled into the page and named by version where you can see it.
  • Standard gravity throughout, and where the two engines disagree the page tells you by how much.
  • Answers follow your edits as you make them, and if a big network makes that slow, it is a project setting you can turn off.

Time 6

  • Extended-period simulation: tanks fill and drain, demands follow their patterns, and a bar along the bottom plays the run or steps to any moment in it.
  • Patterns on a reservoir head and on a pump’s speed, beside the demand patterns.
  • A Libraries box holding the patterns and curves a run needs.
  • Rule-based controls, in EPANET’s own words: read them, write them, and edit them in the Libraries box. Every level, pressure and flow in a rule is put into the units your project is showing.
  • A Run button (optional, since answers can follow your edits instead) shows real progress, a completion report, and the engine’s own report of the run.
  • Colour limits hold still across a run, so a colour means the same thing at every step.

Files and EPANET 5

  • Opens EPANET .inp files, takes the part it supports, and reports every difference instead of dropping it quietly.
  • An import note is filed on the element it concerns, and you read it in that element’s own property box.
  • Writes .inp files back out. Every value you did not edit comes back exactly as it went in, character for character.
  • Projects are .lwn files: JSON inside, on your own disk, readable without us.
  • An examples library that opens Net1, Net2, Net3, Elm Street Center and a Net3 placed on the world, in one click.

The map and the world 4

  • A geographic project is drawn in latitude and longitude over a street map or satellite imagery, with the same drawing tools.
  • An existing x/y network can be placed on the world, moved, scaled and rotated onto its real ground.
  • Type a place name and the map goes there, if you turn that on.
  • Ground elevations read from terrain data and typed into the document (as each node is drawn, or over a set you found), never replacing a number without your having asked for exactly that.

Labels and lettering 7

  • Labels place themselves. Where there is no room, a label sheds values in the order you chose instead of disappearing whole.
  • Any label can be dragged where you want it, and it keeps a leader back to what it names.
  • Pipe labels lie along their pipe, the way a map draws a street name.
  • Prefixes, suffixes and a separator you choose, with a link’s values on one line.
  • A Text object takes several lines, bold, and a rotation that can match a pipe.
  • Lettering is haloed, so it stays readable over the drawing underneath it.
  • Text, symbols and pipes are three independent screen sizes, so a drawing reads the same at every zoom.

Reading the results 4

  • A table for each kind of part in the bottom pane: junctions, reservoirs, tanks, pipes, pumps and valves.
  • Colour the map by any value, from one control: three to seven classes, eight ways of choosing where the breaks fall, and 41 colour ramps.
  • Profiles. Click along the map to choose the path, and see ground, hydraulic grade and the pressure between them.
  • A profile path is saved with the project under a name you give it, and a project holds as many of them as you draw.

Scenarios 1

  • Change a few properties without disturbing the network they belong to, switch between those scenarios, and see at a glance which elements carry an override.

Language, licence, and privacy 4

  • Twenty-seven languages, and every word is evaluated for translation: the network model and the two Manning calculators go into all of them, the rest of the suite into Spanish, Portuguese, French, and Turkish, and every calculator is findable by name in every language while it waits its turn.
  • Free software under the GNU GPL, source in the open.
  • It installs on a desktop or a phone as an app of its own, icon and all.
  • No account, no visitor identifier, and nothing counted about you unless you say yes.

The edges

A list like this reads as though somebody has surveyed the whole territory. Nobody has. We know what we built and we know some of what we lack; we do not know the size of the gap between this and the software an experienced modeller is used to.

What we do know we are missing:

  • A run over time goes through the EPANET engine. The built-in solver has no time dimension.
  • Valves that set their own position, meaning pressure reducing, pressure sustaining and flow control, solve through that engine too. A throttle valve solves in either.
  • With the engine out of reach, a network without such a valve is still solved for one instant and the page says so; one holding a valve is refused by name rather than answered wrongly.

And although you of course prefer working on your PC, it works also on a phone in tall mode.

For your privacy: nothing reaches anyone else unless you turn on a feature that needs it, and each one asks separately. Today there are four: street map tiles from OpenStreetMap; satellite tiles from Mapbox; place-name search, which sends what you typed to OpenStreetMap’s geocoder; and ground elevations, which send the coordinates of your nodes to Mapbox. The last two ask a consent question of their own. Nothing you draw is uploaded, and there is no account to make.

If something you need is not on this list, that is the most useful thing you can tell us. A wish list is a specification.
